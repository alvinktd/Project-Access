Access Class of 2028 Cohort

The cohort is a small group of students we work with through a full application cycle. The pilot holds 3 to 4 students, so every student gets real time and a plan built around their own goals.

How the year works

  1. Spring phase, juniors. Starts [month]. We find your story, sort your activities, build your first school list and practice presenting yourself with honesty and confidence.
  2. Summer. Optional office hours. A good time to draft your personal statement before senior year gets busy.
  3. Fall phase, seniors. Starts in August. We work through the Common App, revise essays, apply to programs like QuestBridge where they fit, prepare for interviews and submit on time.

What you want in a school

Before you look at a single name, write down 5 to 10 qualities you want in a school. That list shapes every choice after it.

Think about size, like a large state school or a small liberal arts college. Think about majors and programs, like strong STEM or study abroad. Think about where you want to live. Add what matters outside class too, like distance from home, a community where you see yourself, and how much aid the school has given in the past.

Build your first list

Write down 10 to 30 schools. A first list is a draft, and more variety is better. Mix Ivy League and state schools, high and low acceptance rates, small and large schools, and different cultures, locations and aid histories.

For each school, note what you like, what you don't, and what sets it apart. Those notes become material for your "why this school" essays. Over time, cut the list to schools you would be happy to attend. Keep a few reaches, several solid chances, and a few you are confident you can get into and afford.

Talk to real people

For each school, find 2 to 3 people tied to it. That could be a professor who teaches a class you want, an alum or a student in your major. Ask for a coffee chat. It is a short, informal conversation where you learn things no website will tell you.

Bring two or three questions, like what surprised them about the school. Send a thank you note the same day. One 15-minute call our founder almost skipped ran two and a half hours and changed how he thought about his future.

The art of storytelling

Admissions officers remember stories, not lists. Write down 5 to 15 stories from your life. Next to each one, name the trait it shows and how you will show it instead of telling it.

If a reader finishes your story without learning something new about you, the story wasted their time. Pick the one that says the most about you for your personal statement. Save the rest for supplements, where a smaller story can answer a smaller question.

Writing your essays

Open with a moment, not a thesis, and put yourself at the center of it. Name exact things, like a 2003 Toyota RAV4 or a pair of 5A drumsticks. Specific details let the reader see the scene.

When you finish a draft, read each sentence. Ask whether it could appear in anyone else's essay. If it could, replace it with something only you could write. Give yourself time to revise, and read each draft out loud. The sentences you stumble over need work.

Extracurriculars

Depth matters more than a long list. Work, caring for siblings and helping with a family business count. They show responsibility, and they shaped who you are. Our founder grilled kebabs at festivals for his mom's catering business, and that work told his story as well as any club could.

For each activity, write down what you did, how long you did it and what changed because you were there. Numbers and results make a short description stand out.

Reframing without lying

You do not need to make anything up, and you should never sugarcoat. Reframing means telling the truth about what you did and showing what it says about you.

A part-time job is more than a paycheck when it taught you to talk with strangers, manage money and keep showing up. The test is simple. Would the person who saw you do it agree with how you described it?

Common App 101

The Common App lets you apply to many colleges through one account. You fill out your profile, family information, activities and personal statement once, then send them to each school on your list.

Paying for college

The sticker price is not the price most students pay. Never cross a school off your list before you know what it would cost your family.

Fill out the FAFSA, and the CSS Profile if a school asks for it, as early as you can [verify which schools]. Use each school's net price calculator for an estimate. When offers arrive, compare what you would pay out of pocket each year, not the size of the scholarship.

Gather your documents early

Keep everything in one safe place before deadlines hit.

  • ID, like your birth certificate, passport and Social Security card.
  • Contact info for you and your guardians.
  • Academic records, like transcripts, test scores and writing samples.
  • Financial aid documents, like tax returns, W-2s and pay stubs for the FAFSA and CSS Profile.
  • Every login for your portals, the Common App, QuestBridge and aid accounts.

International students may need immigration documents and English exam scores too.

Tools that help

Our founder uses Notion to track schools and essays, Google Calendar to block time for each deadline, and Wispr Flow to talk out ideas before writing.

AI can help you brainstorm and catch mistakes. It should never write your essay. Admissions officers want to hear your voice, and your story is the one thing no tool can tell for you.

Asking for help

The adults around you want to help. They cannot help with what they do not know. Ask early and ask clearly. Write a short email that says who you are, what you need and when you need it.

When you ask a teacher for a recommendation letter, ask at least a month before the deadline [verify your school's timeline]. Give them a short list of your goals and a few moments from their class that meant something to you.

Interviews

An interview is a conversation, not a test. Most interviewers want to learn who you are beyond your application.

Practice answers to common questions, like why this school, what you do outside class and a challenge you overcame. Tie each answer to a specific story. Bring two or three questions of your own. Dress the way you would for an important day at school, and send a thank you email within a day.
