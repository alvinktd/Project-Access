College Apps 101

Everything I wish I had in one place when I started applying. I was the first in my family to go through this, so I learned it one question at a time. This guide follows the order I would take if I were starting over, from your first school list to your last interview.

It is free, and you can use it whether or not you ever work with us.

1. Start with what you want in a school

Before you look at a single name, write down 5 to 10 qualities you want in a school. That list shapes every choice after it.

Think about size, like a large state school or a small liberal arts college. Think about majors and programs, like strong STEM or study abroad. Think about where you want to live. Then add what matters outside class. Distance from home. A community where you can see yourself. How much aid the school has given in the past.

2. Build your first list

Write down 10 to 30 schools. A first list is a draft, and more variety is better. Mix Ivy League and state schools, high and low acceptance rates, small and large schools, and different cultures, locations and aid histories.

For each school, note what you like, what you don't, and what sets it apart. Those notes become material for your "why this school" essays.

Over time, cut the list to schools you would be happy to attend. Keep a few reaches, several solid chances, and a few you are confident you can get into and afford.

3. Talk to real people

Conversations take you far. In one week, I was on the phone with an executive at the NBA and with an entrepreneur who builds startups and works at Adobe. Neither call happened by luck. I asked.

Sometimes all you need to do is find a person on LinkedIn, reach out in your own voice, and ask to hear about what they do. A lot of people are willing to say yes. The problem is that not a lot of people are willing to ask.

The summer before my junior year, my mom set up a 15-minute call with a family friend's son. That call ran two and a half hours. It taught me more about investing, networking and financial literacy than any class had.

How to do it

  • For each school on your list, find 2 to 3 people tied to it. A professor who teaches a class you want. An alum. A student in your major.
  • Write a short message. Say who you are, why you are reaching out to them, and ask for 15 minutes.
  • Come with two or three questions. What surprised you about the school? What do you wish you had known as a freshman?
  • Send a thank you note the same day.

Learn from people ahead of you

If you see someone in the position you want to be in, look at what they did. They paved the way. Sometimes all you need to do is take the initiative to look. Some things you learn by experience. Some things you learn by someone else's experience.

4. The art of storytelling

Admissions officers remember stories, not lists. Write down 5 to 15 stories from your life. Next to each one, name the trait it shows and how you will show it instead of telling it.

If a reader finishes your story without learning something new about you, the story wasted their time. Pick the one that says the most about you for your personal statement. Save the rest for supplements, where a smaller story can answer a smaller question.

5. Writing your essays

The goal of your essays is to turn you from one more application into a person. When I wrote mine, I went from applicant number 1, 2, 3 to Alvin Tete-Djawu. The son of a Ghanaian immigrant single mother who worked overnight shifts while running her catering business. The kid who grilled kebabs at festivals for her business. The kid in the 2003 Toyota RAV4, pulling through the school gates behind a line of Teslas and Mercedes.

None of that is a list of awards. It is a picture of a person, and a reader remembers a person.

How to get there

  • Open with a moment, not a thesis. Put yourself at the center of it.
  • Name exact things, like a 2003 Toyota RAV4 or a pair of 5A drumsticks. Specific details let the reader see the scene.
  • Read each sentence and ask whether it could appear in anyone else's essay. If it could, replace it with something only you could write.
  • Give yourself time. My best essays took several drafts.
  • Read each draft out loud. The sentences you stumble over need work.

6. Reframing without lying

Say I played in the World Cup final and scored the game-winning goal for the Ghana national team. Now say all I tell you is that I played in a soccer match and scored a tap-in. You would not understand what I did. I never painted the picture. I never told you about the stadium, the minutes left on the clock, or a country back home holding its breath. Every word was true, and the story was still missing.

That is what most students do with their applications. They shrink what they did until it sounds like nothing.

Reframing fixes that. You do not need to make anything up, and you should never sugarcoat. Reframing means telling the truth about what you did and showing what it says about you. A part-time job is more than a paycheck when it taught you to talk with strangers, manage money and keep showing up.

The test is simple. Would the person who saw you do it agree with how you described it?

7. Extracurriculars

Depth matters more than a long list. Work, caring for siblings and helping with a family business count. They show responsibility, and they shaped who you are. I grilled kebabs at festivals for my mom's catering business, and that work told my story as well as any club could.

For each activity, write down what you did, how long you did it and what changed because you were there. Numbers and results make a short description stand out.

8. Common App 101

The Common App lets you apply to many colleges through one account. You fill out your profile, family information, activities and personal statement once, then send them to each school on your list.

  • The personal statement has a 650-word limit. You choose from a set of broad prompts.
  • The activities section holds up to 10 activities, with a short description of up to 150 characters for each.
  • The additional information section gives context that fits nowhere else, like family responsibilities or a hard year.

Many schools ask their own supplement questions. Make a spreadsheet with each school's essays, word limits and deadlines the day you add it to your list. Check commonapp.org for this year's prompts.

9. Programs to know

Two programs stand out, and I can speak to both. I have either been through them myself or have people close to me in them who can tell you what they are like.

QuestBridge

QuestBridge connects high-achieving, low-income students with partner colleges. Juniors can apply to the College Prep Scholars Program. Seniors can apply to the National College Match. I am a QuestBridge Scholar.

Thrive Scholars

Thrive Scholars supports high-achieving students from low-income backgrounds on their path to and through college.

Also worth a look

  • Upward Bound. A federal program for low-income and first-generation high school students. It gave me weekly sessions and two summers of college prep.
  • Fly-in programs. Many colleges host free visit programs for students who could not afford the trip. Search a school's name with "fly-in program" to find them.

Check each program's website for current rules and deadlines before you apply.

10. Know the real cost

The sticker price is not the price most students pay. Never cross a school off your list before you know what it would cost your family.

Fill out the FAFSA, and the CSS Profile if a school asks for it, as early as you can. Use each school's net price calculator for an estimate. When offers arrive, compare what you would pay out of pocket each year, not the size of the scholarship.

11. Gather your documents early

Keep everything in one safe place before deadlines hit.

  • ID, like your birth certificate, passport and Social Security card.
  • Contact info for you and your guardians.
  • Academic records, like transcripts, test scores and writing samples.
  • Financial aid documents, like tax returns, W-2s and pay stubs for the FAFSA and CSS Profile.
  • Every login for your portals, the Common App, QuestBridge and aid accounts.

International students may need immigration documents and English exam scores too.

12. Tools that help

I use Notion to track schools and essays, Google Calendar to block time for each deadline, and Wispr Flow to talk out ideas before I write.

AI can help you brainstorm and catch mistakes. It should never write your essay. Admissions officers want to hear your voice, and your story is the one thing no tool can tell for you.

13. Asking for help

The adults around you want to help. They cannot help with what they do not know. Ask early and ask clearly. Write a short email that says who you are, what you need and when you need it.

A 20-minute check-in with my college counselor turned into an hour on what I wanted in a school. That hour pointed me to programs I had never heard of. It only happened because I showed up and asked.

When you ask a teacher for a recommendation letter, ask at least a month before the deadline, or earlier if your school sets its own timeline. Give them a short list of your goals and a few moments from their class that meant something to you.

14. Interviews

I have been through quite a few interviews. The biggest lesson is that an interview is a two-way conversation. It is not only for the interviewer to learn about you. It is also for you to learn about the school.

Practice answers to common questions, like why this school, what you do outside class and a challenge you overcame. Tie each answer to a specific story.

The end matters most

One of the most important moments comes at the end, when you get to ask questions. This is your part of the interview. Use it to figure out if this is the place for you. It is also your chance to show you are thoughtful. Asking good questions is a skill most adults never master.

To ask a good question, you have to listen well, process what you hear, notice what you don't know, and work to fill that gap. Listen during the interview, and build your questions from what your interviewer tells you.

Dress the way you would for an important day at school, and send a thank you email within a day.
